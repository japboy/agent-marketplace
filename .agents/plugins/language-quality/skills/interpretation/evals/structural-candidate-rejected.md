# Interpretation

Identify the source and listener. Use the remark or answer the user named, including your own draft
or prior answer when that is the source. Start with the current exchange and consult accessible
relevant history, memory, or references when needed. Ask for the source if you cannot identify it.

## Establish what the speaker said and meant

Read the remark in the context of the exchange, including what the speaker is responding to. Build a
source frame with only what the evidence supports as the speaker's position or request. Keep these
fields distinct and leave a field empty when the evidence gives no support for it.

- **Goal and means.** Identify what the speaker wants to achieve, what they propose or use to
  achieve it, and why that means helps. Carry the supported goal-to-means link into the
  interpretation, including when the speaker explained it earlier.
- **Conditions and status.** Attach each prerequisite, scope limit, or timing detail to the action
  it qualifies. Preserve what the speaker reports, proposes, has decided, or has done.
- **Requested response.** Identify what the speaker wants this listener to do or answer, by when.
  Preserve indirect requests when supported. For a request phrased as a question, preserve the act
  or information the question actually seeks.

When interpreting your own answer, preserve what you found or did and its limits. State an implicit
ask only when your position and the task support it.

## Adapt to the listener

Use the listener's role, knowledge, preferences, next decision or action, and actual reply to choose
terms, background, and what needs clarification. Ask for missing context only if it could change the
interpretation or the listener's next decision. Check who can answer a material question.

The listener's duties, plans, and concerns belong to the listener. The speaker's request and
decision status come from the source frame. A deadline for the listener's own work stays with that
work. When the listener is considering a different action, carry over a speaker's condition only if
its scope covers that action.

## Deliver the interpretation

Give the speaker's message in the listener's language and terms. Attribute the position to the
speaker and keep each supported request tied to the person, action, and deadline it concerns. When
your own answer is the source, speak directly to the user. Use a standalone message for a simple
relay.

If the answer also needs your explanation, question, or advice for the listener's decision, use two
labeled sections in the listener's language, such as **Speaker's message** and **Interpreter's
clarification**. Put any suggested next step in the clarification and make clear whose choice it is.
If the listener reads their own next step as a speaker's request, clarify who owns that step. For a
material uncertainty, give the specific question and name who can resolve it. For uncertain scope,
ask that person whether the condition covers the listener's action. Leave the question open until an
actual answer is available.

## Check and repair understanding

When there is a reply, use what the listener actually says to identify any misunderstanding that
affects the current purpose. Repair that point using the same source frame and keep open questions
with their owners. If repair requires an answer from the speaker, pass on the listener's reading or
question and relay the speaker's actual answer when available. When you are the speaker, answer
directly. A reply can show enough understanding even when the listener disagrees. End the exchange
when the participants understand enough for the current purpose. In a one-way exchange, give the
interpretation and any material clarification that can be stated from the available evidence.
